## Overview

The **Business KPI Library** is an organized reference collection dedicated to understanding how organizations measure performance, evaluate results, identify trends, and support informed business decisions.

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) are measurable values used to evaluate progress toward defined business objectives. They help organizations understand whether their activities, processes, and strategies are producing the intended results.

# 3. What Is a KPI?

A **Key Performance Indicator** is a metric selected to evaluate performance against an important objective.

Not every metric is a KPI.

For example, a business may track website visits, but if its primary objective is increasing online purchases, conversion rate may be more directly relevant.

### Metric Versus KPI

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metric | A measurable quantity | Website visits |
| KPI | A metric tied to an important objective | Website purchase conversion rate |
| Target | Desired performance level | A defined conversion goal |
| Actual | Observed performance | Calculated conversion rate |
| Variance | Difference between actual and target | Actual minus target |

### Important Principle

**The importance of a KPI depends on the business objective it is intended to measure.**

# 11. KPI Formula Reference

The following formulas are introductory examples. Each metric should receive a dedicated resource with precise definitions and calculation rules.

| KPI | Illustrative Formula |
|---|---|
| Sales Growth Rate | `(Current Sales − Previous Sales) ÷ Previous Sales × 100` |
| Average Order Value | `Defined Order Revenue ÷ Number of Orders` |
| Gross Profit | `Net Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold` |
| Gross Profit Margin | `Gross Profit ÷ Net Revenue × 100` |
| Net Profit Margin | `Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100` |
| Current Ratio | `Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities` |
| Return on Ad Spend | `Attributed Advertising Revenue ÷ Advertising Spend` |
| Click-Through Rate | `Clicks ÷ Impressions × 100` |
| Error Rate | `Error-Containing Units ÷ Total Evaluated Units × 100` |

### Important Consideration

All formulas require appropriate denominator checks, consistent definitions, and compatible reporting periods.

# 12. Worked Example: Average Order Value

## Business Question

What is the average revenue generated per order?

### Hypothetical Data

- Total qualifying order revenue: $12,000
- Number of qualifying orders: 300

### Formula

**Average Order Value = Total Qualifying Order Revenue ÷ Number of Qualifying Orders**

### Calculation

$12,000 ÷ 300 = $40

### Expected Result

**Average Order Value = $40 per order**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business generated an average of $40 in revenue per qualifying order during the reporting period.

### Potential Business Application

A retailer could monitor average order value when evaluating product bundles, pricing, or purchasing patterns.

### Limitation

Average order value does not explain profitability, customer satisfaction, or the distribution of order amounts.

---

# 13. Worked Example: Gross Profit Margin

## Business Question

What percentage of net revenue remains after cost of goods sold?

### Hypothetical Data

- Net Revenue: $50,000
- Cost of Goods Sold: $30,000

### Step 1: Calculate Gross Profit

$50,000 − $30,000 = $20,000

### Step 2: Calculate Gross Profit Margin

$20,000 ÷ $50,000 × 100 = 40%

### Expected Result

**Gross Profit Margin = 40%**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business retains 40% of net revenue after deducting cost of goods sold, before accounting for other operating expenses and applicable costs.

### Limitation

Gross profit margin is not equivalent to net profit margin.

---

# 14. Worked Example: Marketing ROAS

## Business Question

How much attributed revenue was generated per dollar of advertising spend?

### Hypothetical Data

- Attributed Advertising Revenue: $8,000
- Advertising Spend: $2,000

### Formula

**ROAS = Attributed Advertising Revenue ÷ Advertising Spend**

### Calculation

$8,000 ÷ $2,000 = 4

### Expected Result

**ROAS = 4.0**, commonly expressed as **4:1**

### Interpretation

The campaign was attributed $4 in revenue for every $1 in advertising spend.

### Limitation

ROAS does not directly measure profit and may be affected by attribution methodology.

# 15. Selecting the Right KPI

KPI selection should begin with the business objective.

### Recommended Process

1. Identify the business objective.
2. Define the decision the metric should support.
3. Identify relevant business activity.
4. Select a measurable indicator.
5. Confirm required data is available.
6. Define the calculation.
7. Establish the reporting period.
8. Determine the appropriate comparison.
9. Identify limitations.
10. Document the KPI definition.

### Example

**Business Objective:** Improve order processing efficiency.

**Potential KPI:** Average Order Processing Time.

**Required Data:** Order receipt timestamp and processing completion timestamp.

**Interpretation:** Evaluate how processing duration changes over time.

### Important Principle

**A useful KPI should help answer a meaningful business question rather than simply add another number to a report.**

# 16. Leading & Lagging Indicators

KPIs may describe activity that precedes an outcome or results that have already occurred.

### Leading Indicators

Measures that may provide early information about future performance.

Examples may include:

- Qualified leads
- Sales pipeline activity
- Order processing backlog

### Lagging Indicators

Measures of results that have already occurred.

Examples may include:

- Revenue
- Net profit
- Customer churn

### Important Consideration

Whether a metric is leading or lagging depends on the business objective and context.

# 18. KPI Variance Analysis

Variance analysis compares actual results with expected or planned values.

### Illustrative Example

- Revenue Target: $20,000
- Actual Revenue: $18,000

### Absolute Variance

$18,000 − $20,000 = −$2,000

### Percentage Variance

−$2,000 ÷ $20,000 × 100 = −10%

### Interpretation

Actual revenue was $2,000, or 10%, below the target.

### Important Consideration

Whether a positive or negative variance is favorable depends on the KPI.

# 20. KPI Data Quality & Validation

Accurate KPI reporting depends on reliable data.

### Common Data Quality Risks

- Missing records
- Duplicate transactions
- Inconsistent dates
- Incorrect categories
- Wrong data types
- Invalid calculations
- Incomplete reporting periods
- Misaligned numerator and denominator
- Incorrect joins

### Recommended Validation Process

1. Confirm source data.
2. Review reporting dates.
3. Check missing values.
4. Investigate duplicates.
5. Verify metric definitions.
6. Recalculate a sample manually.
7. Compare with source totals.
8. Review unusual results.
9. Document assumptions.

### Important Principle

**A KPI should not be considered reliable until its underlying calculation and source data have been validated.**

# 21. KPI Interpretation

A KPI result should be accompanied by a clear explanation.

### Interpretation Framework

**What happened?**

Describe the observed result.

**Compared with what?**

Identify the target, prior period, or benchmark.

**Why might it matter?**

Explain the business relevance.

**What remains uncertain?**

Identify missing information or limitations.

**What should be investigated next?**

Recommend an appropriate follow-up.

### Important Consideration

A KPI trend may indicate an issue or opportunity without establishing its cause.

# 29. Common KPI Mistakes

### Frequent Errors

- Selecting metrics without defined objectives
- Using inconsistent formulas
- Comparing incompatible periods
- Ignoring data quality
- Using incorrect denominators
- Confusing revenue with profit
- Confusing ROAS with ROI
- Overlooking seasonality
- Misinterpreting averages
- Assuming correlation proves causation
- Treating benchmarks as universal
- Reporting results without limitations

### Important Principle

**Accurate KPI reporting requires clear definitions, appropriate calculations, reliable data, and careful interpretation.**

# 30. KPI Resource Documentation Standard

Every dedicated KPI resource should include the following nine sections.

## 1. What It Means

Provide a clear, beginner-friendly definition.

Explain the KPI in plain language and identify what it measures.

## 2. Why It Matters

Describe the business value.

Explain which objectives or decisions the metric may support.

## 3. When to Use It

Identify appropriate business situations.

Explain circumstances where the metric may be misleading or insufficient.

## 4. Formula or Calculation Method

Provide the exact formula or procedure.

Define each variable, unit, reporting period, and important assumption.

## 5. Step-by-Step Example

Use realistic hypothetical or appropriately sourced information.

Show each calculation stage.

## 6. Expected Result

Provide the verified numerical result.

Include appropriate units and rounding.

## 7. How to Interpret the Result

Explain what the output means.

Identify appropriate comparisons and limitations.

## 8. Real-World Business Application

Describe a realistic business scenario.

Explain how the KPI might inform decisions.

## 9. Common Mistakes and Limitations

Identify calculation errors, data quality risks, assumptions, and interpretation limitations.
