Financial analysis helps organizations understand their financial position, operating results, profitability, liquidity, cash flow, and financial risks.

# 3. What Is Financial Analysis?

Financial analysis is the process of examining financial information to understand business performance and financial condition.

### Common Questions

- Is the business profitable?
- Can it meet short-term obligations?
- How efficiently are assets being used?
- How much debt does the business carry?
- Are expenses increasing?
- Is revenue growing?
- Is the business generating cash?
- Are financial results meeting expectations?

### Important Principle

**No single financial metric provides a complete picture of business performance.**

---

# 4. Financial Statements

Financial analysis commonly relies on three major financial statements.

| Statement | Primary Purpose | Key Information |
|---|---|---|
| Income Statement | Measures performance over a period | Revenue, expenses, profit |
| Balance Sheet | Shows financial position at a point in time | Assets, liabilities, equity |
| Cash Flow Statement | Reports cash movements over a period | Operating, investing, financing cash flows |

### Important Consideration

The statements are connected, but they measure different aspects of a business.

**A balance sheet represents a point-in-time financial position, not financial performance over an entire period.**

# 10. Current Ratio

## What It Means

The current ratio compares current assets with current liabilities.

### Formula

**Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

### Hypothetical Example

- Current Assets: $120,000
- Current Liabilities: $60,000

### Calculation

$120,000 ÷ $60,000 = 2.0

### Expected Result

**Current Ratio = 2.0**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business has $2.00 of current assets for every $1.00 of current liabilities.

### Important Consideration

A higher ratio does not automatically indicate stronger financial health because the quality and liquidity of current assets matter.

---

# 11. Quick Ratio

The quick ratio evaluates short-term liquidity using more readily liquid assets.

### Common Formula

**Quick Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents + Marketable Securities + Net Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities**

### Important Consideration

Definitions may vary by source and reporting context.

The calculation should identify which assets are included.

# 13. Debt-to-Equity Ratio

## What It Means

The debt-to-equity ratio compares debt with shareholders' or owners' equity.

### Formula

**Debt-to-Equity Ratio = Total Debt ÷ Total Equity**

### Hypothetical Example

- Total Debt: $80,000
- Total Equity: $100,000

### Calculation

$80,000 ÷ $100,000 = 0.80

### Expected Result

**Debt-to-Equity Ratio = 0.80**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business has $0.80 of debt for every $1.00 of equity.

### Important Consideration

Some analyses use total liabilities instead of interest-bearing debt. The definition must be stated clearly to support valid comparisons.

# 15. Accounts Receivable Turnover

## What It Means

Accounts receivable turnover measures how frequently average receivables are collected relative to net credit sales during a period.

### Formula

**Receivables Turnover = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Net Accounts Receivable**

### Hypothetical Example

- Net Credit Sales: $600,000
- Average Net Accounts Receivable: $100,000

### Calculation

$600,000 ÷ $100,000 = 6

### Expected Result

**Receivables Turnover = 6 times**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business generated net credit sales equal to six times its average net receivables during the reporting period.

### Important Consideration

The measure should be interpreted alongside credit terms, customer mix, collection practices, and the reporting period.

---

# 16. Days Sales Outstanding

Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) estimates the average collection period for receivables.

### Common Formula

**DSO = Average Net Accounts Receivable ÷ Net Credit Sales × Days in Period**

### Hypothetical Example

- Average Net Accounts Receivable: $100,000
- Net Credit Sales: $600,000
- Reporting Period: 365 days

### Calculation

$100,000 ÷ $600,000 × 365 = 60.83 days

### Expected Result

**Approximately 60.83 days**

### Important Consideration

DSO is an estimate based on aggregated financial information and should not be treated as the actual age of every invoice.

# 18. Gross Profit Margin

## What It Means

Gross profit margin measures the percentage of net revenue remaining after cost of goods sold.

### Formula

**Gross Profit Margin = (Net Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Net Revenue × 100**

### Hypothetical Example

- Net Revenue: $50,000
- Cost of Goods Sold: $30,000

### Calculation

($50,000 − $30,000) ÷ $50,000 × 100 = 40%

### Expected Result

**Gross Profit Margin = 40%**

### Interpretation

The fictional business retains 40 cents of gross profit per dollar of net revenue before other operating expenses.

---

# 19. Net Profit Margin

Net profit margin measures net income relative to revenue.

### Formula

**Net Profit Margin = Net Income ÷ Net Revenue × 100**

### Hypothetical Example

- Net Income: $8,000
- Net Revenue: $80,000

### Calculation

$8,000 ÷ $80,000 × 100 = 10%

### Expected Result

**Net Profit Margin = 10%**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business generated $0.10 in net income per dollar of net revenue.

# 21. Contribution Margin

Contribution margin measures revenue remaining after variable costs.

### Formula

**Contribution Margin = Sales Revenue − Variable Costs**

### Per-Unit Formula

**Contribution Margin per Unit = Selling Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit**

### Hypothetical Example

- Selling Price: $50
- Variable Cost: $30

### Calculation

$50 − $30 = $20

### Expected Result

**Contribution Margin per Unit = $20**

### Interpretation

Each unit contributes $20 toward fixed costs and, after fixed costs are covered, profit.

---

# 22. Contribution Margin Ratio

### Formula

**Contribution Margin Ratio = Contribution Margin ÷ Sales Revenue × 100**

### Hypothetical Example

- Contribution Margin: $20
- Selling Price: $50

### Calculation

$20 ÷ $50 × 100 = 40%

### Expected Result

**Contribution Margin Ratio = 40%**

### Interpretation

Forty percent of sales revenue remains after variable costs to cover fixed costs and contribute to profit.

---

# 23. Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis estimates the sales volume required for total revenue to equal total costs.

### Formula

**Break-Even Units = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin per Unit**

### Hypothetical Example

- Fixed Costs: $10,000
- Selling Price per Unit: $50
- Variable Cost per Unit: $30

### Step 1: Calculate Contribution Margin

$50 − $30 = $20

### Step 2: Calculate Break-Even Quantity

$10,000 ÷ $20 = 500 units

### Expected Result

**Break-Even Quantity = 500 units**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical business must sell 500 units to cover its fixed and variable costs under the stated assumptions.

### Limitations

This simplified model assumes constant selling price, constant variable cost per unit, and fixed costs within the relevant activity range.

# 25. Revenue Growth Rate

### Formula

**Revenue Growth Rate = (Current Revenue − Prior Revenue) ÷ Prior Revenue × 100**

### Hypothetical Example

- Prior Revenue: $40,000
- Current Revenue: $46,000

### Calculation

($46,000 − $40,000) ÷ $40,000 × 100 = 15%

### Expected Result

**Revenue Growth Rate = 15%**

### Interpretation

Revenue increased by 15% compared with the prior period.

### Important Consideration

The reporting periods must be comparable, and a zero prior-period value requires separate treatment.

# 27. Budget Variance Analysis

Variance analysis compares actual results with planned or budgeted amounts.

### Formula

**Variance = Actual Amount − Budget Amount**

### Hypothetical Example

- Budgeted Expense: $5,000
- Actual Expense: $5,600

### Calculation

$5,600 − $5,000 = $600

### Expected Result

**Expense Variance = +$600**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical expense exceeded budget by $600.

### Important Consideration

A positive expense variance under this formula is unfavorable when the objective is to remain within budget. Revenue variances require a different interpretation.

---

# 28. Percentage Variance

### Formula

**Percentage Variance = (Actual − Budget) ÷ Budget × 100**

### Hypothetical Example

- Budget: $5,000
- Actual: $5,600

### Calculation

$600 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 12%

### Expected Result

**Percentage Variance = +12%**

### Interpretation

The hypothetical expense exceeded budget by 12%.

---

# 29. Revenue & Expense Mix Analysis

Mix analysis evaluates the relative contribution of categories to a total.

**Marketing Expense Share = 20%**

Marketing represents 20% of total expenses in the hypothetical example.

# 34. Free Cash Flow

A commonly used free cash flow measure evaluates operating cash flow after capital expenditures.

### Common Formula

**Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures**

### Hypothetical Example

- Operating Cash Flow: $50,000
- Capital Expenditures: $15,000

### Calculation

$50,000 − $15,000 = $35,000

### Expected Result

**Free Cash Flow = $35,000**

### Important Consideration

Free cash flow is a non-GAAP measure, and definitions can vary. The calculation method should be disclosed.

# 37. Straight-Line Depreciation

## What It Means

Straight-line depreciation allocates an asset's depreciable cost evenly across its estimated useful life.

### Formula

**Annual Depreciation = (Asset Cost − Estimated Salvage Value) ÷ Useful Life**

### Hypothetical Example

- Asset Cost: $12,000
- Salvage Value: $2,000
- Useful Life: 5 years

### Calculation

($12,000 − $2,000) ÷ 5 = $2,000

### Expected Result

**Annual Depreciation = $2,000**

### Important Consideration

Financial reporting depreciation and tax depreciation may follow different rules.

---

# 39. Prepaid Expenses

A prepaid expense represents a payment for benefits expected to be received in future periods.

### Hypothetical Example

A business pays $1,200 for 12 months of insurance coverage.

### Calculation

$1,200 ÷ 12 = $100 per month

### Expected Result

**Monthly Expense Allocation = $100**

### Important Consideration

This simplified example assumes equal monthly allocation is appropriate for the coverage period.
